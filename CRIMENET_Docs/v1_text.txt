1. Executive Summary

CRIMENET AI turns fragmented, heterogeneous investigation data — FIRs, call detail records (CDRs), financial transactions, surveillance reports, social-media intelligence — into a structured Criminal Intelligence Knowledge Graph, then applies AI/NLP and graph analytics to surface relationships, key players, and suspicious patterns for investigators. It is built as an AI-assisted decision-support tool, not an autonomous decision-maker.

Core Principle

• Extract → Reconcile → Connect → Analyze → Explain → Assist

• — not the naive "Upload → Generate Graph" approach most competing teams will pitch.

Why this stands out

Hybrid AI philosophy: deterministic code, NLP, ML, and graph algorithms each do the job they're best at — the LLM is only used for interaction and evidence-grounded explanation.

Evidence provenance is mandatory for every important relationship — nothing the system claims is un-traceable.

Explicit ethical boundaries (Section 3) — the system never claims to determine guilt.

A working data-and-graph pipeline is the flagship deliverable; the LLM layer is an enhancement, not the dependency.

2. The Problem, In One Page

Investigations today are not blocked by a lack of data — they're blocked by fragmentation. Case-relevant information is scattered across FIRs, police reports, CDRs, bank transaction logs, surveillance notes, and social media, in different formats and systems. Investigators manually stitch together relationships between entities (people, phones, vehicles, accounts, locations, organizations, events) that appear inconsistently across these sources.

Core entities the system must track

Person · Phone Number · Vehicle · Bank Account · Location · Organization · Event · Case

Why this is a fusion problem, not a visualization problem

SIH26189 is best understood as a multisource intelligence fusion and relationship-analysis problem. A graph is the output representation — the hard part is getting clean, resolved, evidence-linked entities and relationships into that graph in the first place.

3. Product Vision & What We Will NOT Claim

CRIMENET AI is positioned as: "A decision-support and intelligence-discovery system that helps authorized investigators understand complex networks while preserving evidence provenance and human oversight."

Explicit non-claims (this is a strength, not a hedge)

That AI can determine guilt.

That graph centrality proves criminal involvement.

That an association automatically means criminal association.

That synthetic-data performance equals field performance.

That AI predictions are infallible.

That every detected relationship is genuine.

That the system replaces investigators.

That the system can autonomously make enforcement decisions.

Keep this section prominent in the pitch deck — for an MHA-sponsored, civil-liberties-adjacent problem statement, judges will actively look for exactly this kind of self-awareness.

6. Investigator UX (Condensed)

The interface is built around a single investigator workflow: Case Workspace → Data Ingestion → Entity Review Queue → Network Explorer (graph + timeline + map) → Evidence Explorer → AI Assistant → Report Generation.

Flagship demo flow (already scripted — presentation-ready)

Scene 1 — Investigator opens a case with an initially incomplete network.

Scene 2 — Uploads 3 heterogeneous sources (FIR PDF, CDR CSV, transactions CSV).

Scene 3 — UI visibly shows AI processing: extracting entities → resolving identities → building relationships → running analytics.

Scene 4 — Graph appears; two initially separate clusters resolve into a shared bridge entity, "Person X" — the flagship discovery moment.

This is your strongest live-demo asset — protect it in scoping (see Section 10).

7. Part 6 — Data Architecture, Knowledge Graph Schema & Database Design

This part defines exactly what CRIMENET stores, how entities connect, and how the application retrieves them. It follows directly from the layered architecture in Section 4 and the entity model established in Parts 1–2.

7.1 Storage Strategy: Why Two Databases

CRIMENET deliberately splits storage by workload, rather than forcing everything into one engine:

Store

Role

Holds

PostgreSQL

System of record

Users, cases, evidence metadata, audit logs, AI job status, permissions — anything relational, transactional, and access-controlled.

Neo4j

Knowledge graph

Person / Organization / Phone / Vehicle / Account / Location / Event nodes and their relationships — anything that is queried by traversal, not by row lookup.

Object Storage

Raw evidence

PDFs, images, documents, and extracted artifacts — the immutable source-of-truth files every graph fact must trace back to.

Redis / Queue

Async layer

Processing jobs, caching, and asynchronous ingestion workflows so long-running NLP jobs don't block the UI.

7.2 Neo4j Node Types

Person — investigation subjects, witnesses, associates

Organization — companies, gangs, fronts

Vehicle — registration numbers, linked movement records

7.3 Core Relationship Types

AFFILIATED_WITH (Person → Organization)

USES (Person → Phone)

OWNS (Person → Account)

COMMUNICATES_WITH (Person → Person, with call/message metadata + confidence)

PARTICIPATED_IN (Person → Event)

OCCURRED_AT (Event → Location)

LINKED_TO (Person → Vehicle)

Every relationship edge carries: confidence score, source evidence ID(s), extraction method (rule-based / NLP / manual), and a created/verified timestamp. This is what makes Decision D3 (evidence provenance) enforceable at the schema level, not just a policy statement.

7.6 Indexing & Query Patterns

Neo4j: composite index on (entity_type, normalized_name) for fast candidate matching during entity resolution.

Neo4j: relationship property index on confidence for filtering low-confidence edges out of default graph views.

PostgreSQL: index on cases.status and evidence.case_id for dashboard queries.

Common query pattern: "shortest path between two entities within a case, filtered to confidence > threshold" — this should be a first-class, pre-optimized query, since it's the core of the flagship demo discovery moment.

7.7 Retention, Backup & Synthetic Dataset Hook

Evidence files: immutable once uploaded; retention policy configurable per case (align with MHA data-handling norms — flag as an open compliance question in Q&A, don't guess an answer).

Backup: nightly PostgreSQL dump + Neo4j snapshot; both versioned so a demo environment can be reset to a known-good state (needed for the Judge-Facing Demo Mode already scoped in Part 4).

The synthetic dataset (see Section 9) should be loaded through the exact same ingestion → NLP → resolution → graph pipeline as real data — never inserted directly into Neo4j. This is what makes the demo pipeline itself the thing being judged, not a canned graph.

9. Synthetic Data Strategy (Fixed Gap)

This was the single most under-specified risk across Parts 1–5 relative to how much the demo depends on it. Below is a concrete design.

9.1 Ground-truth generator design

Start from a small hand-authored 'true' network (e.g., 40–60 entities, 2 clusters bridged by 1–2 connector entities) — this is your answer key.

Generate source documents (synthetic FIR text, CDR rows, transaction rows) that describe this true network — using varied phrasing, partial name matches, and inconsistent formatting across sources.

Deliberately inject realistic noise: misspelled names, same person with 2 different phone numbers, duplicate near-identical entities, a few red herrings unconnected to the main cluster, missing fields.

Keep the answer key (true entities + true edges) separate — this becomes your evaluation set for entity resolution and relationship extraction accuracy (already scoped in Part 5, Sections 63–66).

9.2 Why this matters more than it looks

Your differentiation (Section 4, Technical Differentiation) rests on entity resolution and evidence-linked relationship extraction actually working — not just a graph rendering nicely. A synthetic dataset that's too clean will make judges suspicious that the hard part isn't real; a synthetic dataset with realistic noise, paired with a visible confidence/review UI, proves the hard part is solved.

9.3 Practical build order

Week 1: hand-author the 40–60 entity ground-truth network + answer key (spreadsheet is fine).

Week 1–2: script-generate the 3 source documents (FIR text, CDR CSV, transactions CSV) from the ground truth, with noise injection parameters you control.

Ongoing: re-run the same generator with different noise seeds to stress-test entity resolution before the final demo.

12. Overall Assessment & Recommendations

Overall take

This is well above typical SIH prep quality. The framing discipline (Extract → Reconcile → Connect → Analyze → Explain → Assist), the explicit ethical non-claims, the hybrid-AI philosophy, and the named architecture risks with mitigations all show mature systems thinking — not just an AI feature bolted onto a graph viewer. The single biggest gap across the original 5 parts was that everything was a specification, not yet a build plan; Sections 7, 9, and 10 of this document exist specifically to close that gap.

Top 5 recommendations, in priority order

Protect the flagship demo (Section 6) above everything else — build backward from it, not forward from the architecture.

Build the synthetic data generator (Section 9) in week 1, not as an afterthought — your whole credibility rests on it.

Cut scope honestly using Section 10 before your team writes a line of backend code — 5 documents' worth of architecture is not 5 documents' worth of hackathon build time.

Keep the 'What We Will NOT Claim' framing (Section 3) visible in the pitch deck itself, not just the docs — it's a genuine differentiator with an MHA-sponsored, civil-liberties-sensitive problem statement.

Rehearse a recorded backup of the full flagship demo flow — live graph demos are the single most common hackathon failure point.

Final note

The architecture is sound and the differentiation story is genuinely strong. The risk isn't the idea — it's execution discipline under a hard deadline. Everything in Sections 9–11 exists to convert your excellent planning into something your team can actually finish and defend live.
